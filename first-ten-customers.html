Find your first ten customers

Your first customers usually come from focused conversations and a clear offer—not from trying every marketing channel at once.

Choose one customer group

Describe one type of person or business that is most likely to need your offer. Consider their role, situation, location, budget, and the moment when the problem becomes urgent.

A narrow starting group makes it easier to find people, understand their language, and explain why your offer matters. You can broaden later after you learn what works.

Make the offer easy to understand

Use one sentence: “I help [specific customer] achieve [useful outcome] without [common frustration].” Avoid vague claims such as “best quality” or “revolutionary.”

Be clear about what is included, the expected result, the price or price range, the delivery time, and what the customer needs to do next. Never promise results you cannot control.

Start with warm, relevant outreach

Make a list of people you already know who may fit the customer group, plus local organizations, professional communities, and online groups where your intended customers already gather.

Send personal, respectful messages. Explain why you are reaching out, what problem you help with, and ask whether a short conversation would be useful. Do not spam, scrape contact lists, or add people to marketing emails without permission.

Demonstrate value before asking for a sale

Share a useful sample, short walkthrough, checklist, before-and-after example, or small piece of advice related to the problem. Keep it genuinely helpful and relevant.

For a service, offer a clearly scoped pilot. For a product, let people see how it works and what it costs. A real demonstration helps customers decide and gives you feedback.

Ask for referrals and learn from every conversation

When someone is satisfied, ask whether they know one other person who might benefit. Make it easy for them to forward a short description of your offer.

Track where each conversation came from, what questions people asked, how many requested more information, and how many bought. If people are interested but do not purchase, ask what is holding them back—price, timing, trust, or a mismatch in the offer.

A simple first-customer routine

Each week, identify ten relevant prospects, start five thoughtful conversations, publish one genuinely useful demonstration or tip, and follow up with people who asked you to. Adjust the numbers to fit your capacity and the buying cycle.

Focus on learning and serving customers well. Ten customers are not a guaranteed outcome or a fixed timeline; they are a practical early milestone for testing whether your offer can attract and retain buyers.

Key takeaway

Get close to one customer group, make one clear offer, and build trust through useful conversations and reliable delivery.
