02 / PRICE · 6 MIN READ

Understand your costs and margins

A price that covers the materials is not necessarily a profitable price. Build a simple picture of what each sale costs and what your business must pay every month.

Separate one-time, recurring, and per-sale costs

One-time startup costs may include equipment, initial setup, branding, or registration fees. Recurring costs may include software, insurance, hosting, rent, and subscriptions. Per-sale costs rise as you sell more: materials, packaging, shipping, payment processing, or contractor time.

Keep these categories separate. A business can look profitable on each sale and still run short of cash because recurring bills and startup costs were forgotten.

Calculate the cost to deliver one sale

Add the costs that directly happen because you make one product or deliver one service. Include materials, packaging, shipping you pay, platform or payment fees, and paid labor.

For services, include a realistic value for your time. If a job takes three hours, the labor cost should reflect those three hours even if you are not paying yourself yet. Otherwise, the price may hide how much work the business really requires.

Understand gross profit and gross margin

Gross profit per sale is selling price minus direct cost. Gross margin is gross profit divided by selling price, multiplied by 100.

Example: if a product sells for $40 and costs $18 to deliver, gross profit is $22. Gross margin is $22 ÷ $40 = 55%. This is before overhead, taxes, and other business expenses.

Use a pricing formula carefully

A simple starting formula is: price = direct cost ÷ (1 − target gross margin). If direct cost is $18 and your target gross margin is 55%, the calculation is $18 ÷ 0.45 = $40.

This is a planning tool, not a command to charge that price. Check what customers value, what alternatives cost, and whether the offer is meaningfully different. A price that works on paper still needs to make sense in the market.

Check the whole business

Estimate monthly overhead and divide it by the number of sales you realistically expect. This gives you a rough view of how much each sale must contribute toward fixed expenses.

Also plan for refunds, discounts, slow months, taxes, compliance costs, and cash-flow timing. Keep a small record of actual costs and update your estimates as invoices and sales arrive.

Key takeaway

Know your direct cost, gross margin, overhead, and realistic sales volume before deciding whether a price is sustainable.
