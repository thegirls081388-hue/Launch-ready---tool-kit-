Validate the problem before building

Before spending weeks building a product or polishing a website, check that a real group of people has a problem they care enough to solve.

Start with the problem, not your solution

Write down the problem in one plain sentence: “People who ___ struggle to ___ because ___.” Be specific about who experiences it and when it happens.

An idea can sound exciting to you and still fail to matter to customers. Your first job is to learn whether the problem is frequent, frustrating, costly, or important enough that someone already spends time or money trying to solve it.

Talk to people who might buy

Find five to ten people who fit your intended customer group. Ask about their recent experiences rather than asking whether they like your idea.

Useful questions include: “When did this last happen?”, “What did you do about it?”, “What was frustrating?”, “What does that solution cost you in time or money?”, and “What have you already tried?”

Avoid leading questions such as “Would you buy my product?” People often want to be encouraging. Their actual behavior is more informative than a polite yes.

Look for evidence of demand

Listen for repeated details. Are several people describing the same problem in their own words? Are they already paying for a workaround, using spreadsheets, hiring help, or spending hours on a task?

Strong signals include a customer agreeing to a follow-up, trying a simple prototype, joining a clearly described waitlist, or paying for a small pilot. Compliments and social-media likes are weaker evidence.

Run a small test

Create the smallest honest version of the offer: a sample, demo, one-page description, short service trial, or manual version of the result. Explain exactly what it does and does not include.

Set a simple test before you begin. For example: “I will speak with eight target customers. If at least four independently describe this problem and two agree to try the pilot, I will continue testing.” The right threshold depends on the price and type of business; this is a learning target, not a universal rule.

Decide what to do next

If evidence is weak, change the customer group, problem, or offer and test again. If the problem is real but your solution is not compelling, ask what outcome people actually need.

Keep notes on what people did, not just what they said. Validate one important assumption at a time, and avoid major spending until you have evidence that customers want the result.

Key takeaway

A validated idea is not one people merely praise. It is one supported by repeated customer problems and meaningful actions.
